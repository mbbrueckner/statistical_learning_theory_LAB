\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A central promise of machine learning is prediction \emph{beyond} the observed
data, and standard regressors are remarkably bad at it: a polynomial diverges at
a rate dictated by its degree, a random forest predicts a constant outside the
convex hull of the training inputs, and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
extrapolates linearly. All three interpolate the data in Figure~\ref{fig:demo}
almost perfectly, yet fail immediately outside the training domain. Symbolic
regression (SR) offers a way out: if the data-generating process \emph{is} a
closed-form formula $f$, recovering $f$ up to symbolic equivalence yields exact
extrapolation by construction. The price is a hard combinatorial search over
expression space~\citep{DongZhongSurvey}, and when no formula in that language
fits the data, SR has no advantage at all (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\begin{figure}[!b]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{../results/figs/demo.pdf}
  \caption{Extrapolation on $f(x)=0.5x\sin x+0.1x^2$ ($128$ noise-free training
  points on $[-5,5]$). The baselines fit the training domain but fail outside
  it; ours recovers the formula exactly and coincides with the dashed truth.}
  \label{fig:demo}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
Let $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ be an unknown formula over a known operator
alphabet. We observe $N_{\mathrm{train}}$ samples $(x_i, y_i)$, where $x_i \sim
\mathrm{Unif}([-a,a]^d)$ and $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i
\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. The goal is a model $\hat f$ minimizing the error
on test points sampled \emph{strictly outside} the training domain, $x \sim
\mathrm{Unif}([-b,b]^d \setminus [-a,a]^d)$, $b > a$:
\begin{equation}
  \mse_{\mathrm{test}} \;=\; \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}
  \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{test}}} \bigl(\hat f(x_i) - f(x_i)\bigr)^2 .
  \label{eq:msetest}
\end{equation}

\begin{sloppypar}
We contribute (i) an SR algorithm that makes the unbiased search
of~\citet{Kahlmeyer} systematic and adds two components aimed at extrapolation,
namely a polynomial background solved in closed form by variable projection and
cross-validation over \emph{extrapolation horizons} for model selection, and
(ii) experiments on dice-rolled formulas mapping out where the method beats
polynomial, random-forest and MLP baselines and where it breaks.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\paragraph{Related work.}
SR systems search by genetic programming~\citep{PySR}, gradient descent through
operator layers~\citep{NeuralSR,SymbolNet}, or continuous
relaxation~\citep{ParFam}, all biasing the search~\citep{DongZhongSurvey} in
ways that are hard to check~\citep{Kahlmeyer}. We adopt the assumption-free but
randomized stance of~\citet{Kahlmeyer} and make it \emph{systematic}.

\subsection{Model assumptions}
\label{sec:model}

We search over \emph{skeletons}: expression trees whose internal nodes carry
operators from $\mathcal{U} = \{\sin,\cos,\exp,\log,\sqrt{\cdot},(\cdot)^2\}$
and $\mathcal{B} = \{+,-,\times,\div\}$ and whose leaves are input variables
$x_j$ or constant placeholders $c$; a skeleton $u$ with $p$ placeholders defines
a parametric family $u_\theta$, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Following
\citet{Kahlmeyer}, the only structural assumption on the search space is that it
contains \emph{small} expressions: the \emph{core complexity} $k(u)$ is the
number of operator nodes, and the search proceeds in order of increasing $k$.
Operators $\log|t|$ and $\sqrt{|t|}$ are \emph{protected}; division is not, so
skeletons may have poles and invalid evaluations are penalized during fitting.

A skeleton becomes a regressor through a linear \emph{polynomial background},
\begin{equation}
  \hat f(x) \;=\; \alpha\, u_\theta(x) \;+\; \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m} \beta_j\, m_j(x),
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with fixed monomials $\{m_j\} = \{1\} \cup \{x_j^q : q \le \mathrm{deg}\}$ (plus
$x_0x_1$ for $d=2$) and $\mathrm{deg} \in \{2,3\}$, fixed by cross-validation
in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
Generalizing linear scaling~\citep{Keijzer} from $\{1\}$ to a monomial basis,
the background is the crucial ingredient: the core $x\sin x$ explains
Figure~\ref{fig:demo} up to a polynomial trend, so with~\eqref{eq:model} it is
the best of the $69$ skeletons at $k=2$ at machine precision, while without it
it ranks $24$th and is pruned. The \emph{total complexity} is $C = k(u) +
\#\{j \ge 2: \beta_j \neq 0\}$, core operators plus active background terms.

\subsection{Parameter learning}
\label{sec:learning}

For a fixed skeleton the parameters split into a nonlinear $\theta$ and a linear
$(\alpha,\beta)$, which we exploit by \emph{variable projection}: for given
$\theta$ the optimal linear coefficients are the least-squares solution
\begin{equation}
  (\alpha^*,\beta^*)(\theta) \;=\; \arg\min_{\alpha,\beta}
  \bigl\lVert y - \alpha\, u_\theta(X) - M\beta \bigr\rVert_2^2,
  \qquad M_{ij}=m_j(x_i),
  \label{eq:varpro}
\end{equation}
computed in closed form. Substituting back leaves a nonlinear least-squares
problem in $\theta$ only, $\min_\theta \lVert r(\theta)\rVert_2^2$ with
$r(\theta) = y - \alpha^*(\theta)\,u_\theta(X) - M\beta^*(\theta)$, solved by a
trust-region solver (finite-difference Jacobians) from the starts $\theta^{(0)}
= \mathbf{1}$ and one draw from $\mathcal{N}(0, 2^2 I)$ (two for $p\ge3$).
Non-finite predictions are mapped to a large constant and skeletons with $p > 6$
placeholders discarded. Selected models are \emph{sparsified}: terms with
$|\beta_j|\,\mathrm{std}(m_j) < 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{std}(y)$ are dropped and the
linear part refitted.

\subsection{Search: exhaustive enumeration, then a diverse beam}
\label{sec:search}

Semantically equivalent skeletons waste budget, so trees are mapped to a
canonical representative: constant subtrees fold into a placeholder ($\sin(c)
\to c$), associative chains are flattened, sorted and allowed at most one
constant term, parameter-absorbing compositions are rejected ($t-c$, $t/c$,
$e^{t+c}$, $\log|ct|$, $(ct)^2$, $t\cdot t$, $t/t$, \dots), and aliases of the
absolute value are kept only as $\sqrt{t^2}$. For $d=1$ this leaves $1, 6, 69,
890, 12\,769$ skeletons with $k = 0,\dots,4$. Such rules only remove duplicates
they can \emph{prove}, but many more are redundant with respect
to~\eqref{eq:model}: since $\alpha$ and the intercept are free, cores agreeing
up to an affine transform of their output are interchangeable, and with the full
background so are cores differing by a background polynomial. An
\emph{evaluation fingerprint} detects this: each skeleton is evaluated on $24$
probe points for $3$ generic parameter draws, the monomial basis is projected
out (\emph{background}) or not (\emph{affine}), and the normalized result is
hashed; a skeleton whose fingerprint was seen is skipped without fitting. Being
heuristic, the filter is validated in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

Search runs in two phases (Algorithm~\ref{alg:search}). Phase~1 fits
\emph{every} canonical skeleton up to $k_{\mathrm{ex}}$, an unbiased search in
the sense of~\citet{Kahlmeyer} that places the method in the
\emph{deterministic} branch of the taxonomy of~\citet{DongZhongSurvey}, whose
trade-off -- global optimality at exponentially growing cost -- is the behaviour
we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:main}. Phase~2 grows skeletons one operator at a
time with a beam, expanding a parent into all canonical trees obtained by
replacing a leaf with a depth-one subtree or combining any node with a fresh
leaf or unary wrapper. Since ranking parents by training loss alone makes the
beam greedy, parents are chosen \emph{round-robin across root-operator groups}
in order of loss, plus $5$ random survivors ($\varepsilon$-greedy); constants
are fitted on a $64$-point subsample during the search and refitted afterwards.
The output is a \emph{Pareto front} $\mathcal{F} = \{\hat f_k\}$: the best model
per core complexity $k \le k_{\max}$, kept only where the training loss strictly
improves.

\begin{algorithm}[!tb]
\caption{Complexity-ordered skeleton search; both loops stop once
$\min \mathcal{F} < 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Var}(y)$.}
\label{alg:search}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $(X,y)$, subsample $(X_s,y_s)$, operators $(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B})$, limits $k_{\mathrm{ex}},k_{\max}$, width $B$
\State $\mathcal{F} \gets \emptyset$; \quad $L_{-1} \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{$\mathcal{F}$: best model per core complexity}
\For{$k = 0,\dots,k_{\max}$}
  \If{$k \le k_{\mathrm{ex}}$} $L_k \gets$ all canonical skeletons with $k$ operator nodes \Comment{Phase 1}
  \Else\ $P \gets$ round-robin top-$B$ of $L_{k-1}$ by loss across root operators $\cup$ 5 random;
  \State \quad $L_k \gets$ canonical one-operator expansions of $P$ (deduplicated) \Comment{Phase 2}
  \EndIf
  \State \Call{Fit}{$u,X_s,y_s$} $\forall u \in L_k$; update $\mathcal{F}$
\EndFor
\State refit $\mathcal{F}$ on all training points; sparsify; \Return Pareto-optimal $\mathcal{F}$
\Procedure{Fit}{$u,X,y$}\Comment{variable projection, Section~\ref{sec:learning}}
  \State $\theta^\star \gets \arg\min_\theta \lVert r(\theta)\rVert_2^2$ (multi-start trust-region NLS), $r(\theta) = y - P(\theta)P(\theta)^{+}y$, $P(\theta) = [\,u_\theta(X)\mid M\,]$
  \State \Return $\theta^\star$, $(\alpha,\beta) = P(\theta^\star)^{+}y$, loss $\lVert r(\theta^\star)\rVert_2^2/N$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Model selection by extrapolation-shaped CV}
\label{sec:selection}

The front $\mathcal{F}$ ranges from underfit to overfit models, and picking by
training loss would always return the most complex one. Because our target
is~\eqref{eq:msetest} rather than interpolation error, we validate the way we
will be tested: training points are ranked by their sup-norm distance from the
domain center, and for each quantile $q \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8\}$ a fold
refits each candidate's constants on the inner $q$ fraction and validates on the
outer ring, giving cross-validation over \emph{extrapolation horizons}. With
$\mathrm{cv}_{k,f}$ the validation MSE of $\hat f_k$ on fold $f \in F$,
candidates are scored by $s_k = \tfrac{1}{|F|}\sum_f \log \mathrm{cv}_{k,f}$ and
the winner is chosen by the one-standard-error rule~\citep[\S7.10.1]{ESL}: among
all $k$ with $s_k \le s_{k^\dagger} + \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(s_{k^\dagger})$,
$k^\dagger$ the score minimizer, the one with smallest $C_k$ is selected and
refitted on all training data. This is robust on training-loss plateaus, where
CV differences are mostly noise. Baseline hyperparameters~\citep[Ch.~3, 11,
15]{ESL} are tuned on the \emph{same} folds, so the comparison is fair.

\paragraph{Limits of the model.}
\label{sec:limits}
The hypothesis space is bounded by $k_{\max}$ ($7$ core operators for $d=1$) and
by the beam: formulas whose subterms only pay off jointly (e.g.\
$e^{-c|x|}\cos(cx)$, $k=7$) can be missed though representable. Protected $\log$
and $\sqrt{\cdot}$ extend past their natural domains~\citep{Keijzer},
unprotected division admits poles that make fitting ill-conditioned, and
constant fitting is non-convex, where multi-starts mitigate but do not eliminate
local minima ($\sin$ frequencies are the classic failure). The background
finally biases the method towards ``scaled core $+$ polynomial'' structure, from
which $\sin(x_0x_1)\log|x_0|$ gains nothing.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Setup.}
\label{sec:setup}
Ground truths are sampled by a dice roll: a random tree with a prescribed number
of operator nodes over one of three alphabets ($\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{poly}} =
\{+,-,\times,(\cdot)^2\}$, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{trig}} =
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{poly}} \cup \{\sin,\cos\}$, and $\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{full}}$
with all ten operators), with leaves $x_j$ (probability $0.7$) or constants from
$\pm\,\mathrm{Unif}([0.2, 3])$. On a dense grid over $[-8,8]^d$ a formula is
rejected if it is invalid, unbounded ($\max|f| > 10^4$), degenerate
($\mathrm{std}(f) < 10^{-3}$), independent of a variable, or affine despite $\ge
3$ dice operators. Since dice-rolled trees often simplify ($x\,(x - x - x)^2 =
x^3$), analyses bin by the \emph{effective complexity} $\eff$, the operator
count of the \texttt{sympy}-simplified formula. Datasets use
$N_{\mathrm{train}} = 128$, $N_{\mathrm{test}} = 256$, $a = 5$, $b = 8$ and
$\sigma = \sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\mathrm{std}(f(X))$. We report the normalized
test error $\nmse = \mse_{\mathrm{test}} / \mathrm{Var}(y_{\mathrm{test}})$,
aggregated by medians since single blow-ups dominate means ($\nmse = 1 \approx$
predicting a constant), and the \emph{recovery rate}~\citep{Kahlmeyer}: the
fraction of runs matching the truth on that grid with relative RMSE below
$10^{-6}$. Baselines are polynomial ridge regression (degree $\le 8$), random
forest and an MLP, all tuned on the folds of Section~\ref{sec:selection}; the
two commands in \texttt{README.md} reproduce all (seeded) numbers.

\paragraph{Search configuration.}
\label{sec:config}
Search hyperparameters are fixed by cross-validation on the folds of
Section~\ref{sec:selection}, never on test data, using a one-factor-at-a-time
grid over $48$ hard datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). Two factors show a
consistent trend: deeper enumeration pays off, but only together with
fingerprint deduplication ($70.8\%$ recovery versus $66.7\%$ at $55\%$ of the
compute), and the background is decisive, with recovery non-decreasing in its
degree and plain linear scaling ten times slower; a wider beam ($B{=}40$) buys
almost nothing at double the cost. Since no configuration dominates, the final
method runs a \emph{portfolio} of the two best, $(k_{\mathrm{ex}}{=}4$, dedup$)$
and $(\mathrm{deg}{=}3)$, keeping the model with the better cross-validated
score. This reaches $79.2\%$, the oracle rate of the full grid at a ninth of its
cost, because an exactly recovered formula drives its cross-validated error to
numerical zero, a signal no wrong configuration can imitate.

\subsection{Extrapolation error and recovery}
\label{sec:main}

\begin{figure}[!tb]
  \centering
  {\footnotesize\input{../results/tab_main}}\\[1.2ex]
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{../results/figs/main.pdf}
  \caption{Main experiment. \emph{Top}: median $\nmse_{\mathrm{test}}$
  ($10^{-30}$ is exact recovery, $1$ is as bad as predicting a constant) and SR
  recovery rate, from $25$ ($d{=}1$) resp.\ $15$ ($d{=}2$) noise-free formulas
  per alphabet--complexity cell. \emph{Bottom} ($d{=}1$, $300$ formulas,
  resolved by the effective complexity $\eff$): (a)~runs with
  $\nmse_{\mathrm{test}} < 10^{-3}$, (b)~runs with exact recovery.}
  \label{fig:main}
\end{figure}

The median $\nmse_{\mathrm{test}}$ of our method is at machine precision for
\emph{every} alphabet (Figure~\ref{fig:main}, top), since in most runs the
formula is recovered exactly and exact recovery implies exact extrapolation:
$85\%$ of the $300$ ground truths are recovered ($100/82/73\%$ for
$\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{poly}}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{trig}}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{full}}$),
$88\%$ of runs reach $\nmse < 10^{-3}$ and $90\%$ reach $\nmse < 0.1$. The
baselines confirm the failure modes from Section~\ref{sec:intro}: the random
forest \emph{never} reaches $\nmse < 10^{-3}$, the MLP succeeds in $1\%$ of runs
and the polynomial in $51\%$ -- almost exactly the fraction of truths that are
themselves polynomials of degree $\le 8$; elsewhere it diverges. The aggregate hides the method's most characteristic property: error
and runtime are \emph{bimodal}. The $75$th percentile of $\nmse_{\mathrm{test}}$
is at machine precision while the $90$th is $0.13$, and the median runtime of
$0.03$\,s contrasts with a worst case of $4.6$\,min on a fruitless full-depth
search -- either the law is recovered or it is missed, and the portfolio pays
its $2.3\times$ overhead mostly where it fails anyway.

The lower panels resolve this by ground-truth complexity: recovery decays from
$99\%$ at $\eff \le 2$ over $93\%$ and $81\%$ to $41\%$ at $\eff \ge 7$. This is
the \emph{complexity wall} of the bounded, partially greedy search, precisely
the exponential-cost trade-off that \citet{DongZhongSurvey} attribute to this
method family; it is steeper for alphabets with $\log$, $\exp$ and division,
which create poles and near-degenerate constants. For $d{=}2$ recovery is $89\%$
overall but drops from $100\%$ at $\eff \le 4$ to $69\%$ beyond, since the
larger leaf set inflates every enumeration level; the baselines look less
catastrophic there (MLP median $\nmse$ $0.09$) because most test-ring points are
extreme in only \emph{one} coordinate, so that task is partly
near-interpolation.

\subsection{Noise and the selection criterion}
\label{sec:noise}

Under label noise (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}), \emph{exact} recovery drops to
zero for any $\sigma > 0$, since the fitted constants inherit an $O(\sigma)$
error, so the interesting quantity is the test error. Our median $\nmse$
degrades gracefully from $0$ to $0.017$ at $\sigma_{\mathrm{rel}} = 0.2$, while
every baseline sits between $0.3$ and $6.6$. Near-perfect extrapolations ($\nmse
< 10^{-3}$) fall from $76\%$ to $12\%$ over that range, but usable ones ($\nmse
< 0.1$) only from $92\%$ to $56\%$: noise degrades the \emph{precision} of the
recovered law much faster than its \emph{shape}.

An ablation of the selection step alone (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}; all
strategies pick from the \emph{same} front) separates two effects at $10\%$
noise. \emph{Parsimony} is large and unambiguous: cross-validated selection
returns a strictly simpler model than training-MSE selection on $92$ of $120$
datasets and never a more complex one (median $C = 6$ versus $9$, $p \approx
6\cdot 10^{-17}$), though its effect on accuracy is far more modest (median
error $0.025$ versus $0.045$, $p = 0.043$). The \emph{fold geometry} has no
measurable effect ($23$ wins, $24$ losses, $73$ ties, $p = 0.63$): both
geometries pick the \emph{identical} model in $61\%$ of cases, because the
one-standard-error rule collapses the decision onto the complexity tie-break
long before fold differences matter. We keep them as the conceptually right
validation at no cost; what earns its keep is parsimony.

\subsection{Analysis of learned models}
\label{sec:analysis}

Recovery is judged \emph{functionally}, and the search often returns an
equivalent but different form: $\sin(2x) - \sin(2x + 2.4)$ is found as
$3.73\cos(x)\sin(x - 0.37) + 0.68$, and for the cubic $1.61 + 1.23(x - x^2)(1.08
- x)$ the core carries only $x^3$ and the background the rest, the division of
labour intended by~\eqref{eq:model}. The $30/300$ runs with $\nmse > 0.1$ show
three patterns: \emph{poles} ($1/(x - 1.32 + \cos x)$), \emph{nested
non-polynomial arguments} ($\log(x - \log(x^2/1.46) - 2.05)$, reachable only
through intermediates that fit poorly alone), and \emph{frequency fitting}
($\sin(x + 2.83x^2)$, where the right skeleton is found but the nonconvex
constant fit misses the frequency). Selection then falls back to a simple,
poorly fitting but \emph{bounded} model, a consequence of validating on
extrapolation horizons.

Cross-validation has one blind spot, since extrapolation-shaped folds still only
extrapolate to the edge of the data: the worst dataset selected $\log(e^{-x} -
e^{e^{x}})$, unremarkable on the training box but $\nmse = 5\cdot 10^{11}$
outside it. A \emph{growth guard} dropping candidates that explode on the box
dilated by $1.5$ closes it, lowering the study's worst case to $153$ and leaving
the other $299$ selections bit-identical. Under noise the danger is the
opposite: above the elbow the front runs flat, decorating the correct core with
negligible terms (e.g.\ $+\,0.0088/\sqrt{|x|}$) that no fit-based criterion
rejects but that destroy \emph{symbolic} recovery. The one-standard-error rule
picks the elbow, returning $0.504\,x\sin x + 0.102x^2$ at $5\%$ noise.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We built a symbolic regressor around three ideas: an unbiased
search~\citep{Kahlmeyer} made systematic by complexity-ordered enumeration over
canonical skeletons, a polynomial background projected out in closed form that
makes partial explanations visible early, and model selection validating the way
the model will be used. It recovers $85\%$ of dice-rolled truths exactly and
beats the baselines by orders of magnitude; their failure is structural and no
tuning fixes it. Not every component earned its
keep equally: the background and parsimony are decisive, the
extrapolation-shaped folds \emph{not} measurably better than interior ones, and
deep enumeration only pushed the complexity wall back. More fundamentally, SR
pays off only if the data-generating process \emph{is} a formula over the chosen
alphabet; otherwise a black box is preferable. Natural extensions: variable
augmentation~\citep{Kahlmeyer}, core-term deletion tests, and neural
guidance~\citep{ParFam} of the beam.
